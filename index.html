HALT — Urge Triage

Run this 10-second check first. Four states drive most unplanned urges.

Select any states that apply to see tailored suggestions.
Hungry

Physical hunger depletes the prefrontal cortex's capacity for self-regulation — the same system you need to resist an urge. Before anything else, eat something. Even a small snack shifts your neurological baseline. The urge may soften significantly once blood sugar stabilizes.

Angry or Anxious

Anger and anxiety both activate threat-response systems that crave immediate relief. Physical discharge helps first: a fast walk, cold water on your face, or five slow exhales. Then use the ABC tool to map what's underneath it.

Lonely

Contact one person — a text is enough. Attend a SMART meeting (online meetings run around the clock). If contact isn't immediately possible, the self-compassion tools in Worksheets are your bridge.

Tired

Sleep deprivation directly impairs impulse control. If you can sleep, do. If not, use DENTS (Tasks or Swap) to occupy your mind until you can rest. Postpone difficult decisions until after you've slept.

Guided Practice
Urge Surfing

Don't fight the urge — observe it. Urges peak and fall in minutes. Work through all six steps.

Step 1 of 6 — Notice
Acknowledge the urge without judgment. Say to yourself: "I notice I'm having an urge." Don't fight it, don't catastrophize it. Just name it. Take a slow breath before pressing Next.
Step 2 of 6 — Locate
Find where you feel it in your body. Scan from head to toe. Where is the physical sensation? Chest tightness? Stomach tension? Restlessness in your legs? Locate it precisely — don't evaluate it, just find it.
Step 3 of 6 — Describe
Describe the sensation as if to a scientist. What is its size, shape, temperature, texture, weight? Is it moving or still? Use neutral language. You're reporting data, not experiencing a crisis.
Step 4 of 6 — Watch the Wave
Observe the urge intensify — and then recede. You're now at or near the peak. Keep breathing. Watch the sensation without adding narrative to it. It will crest. It will fall.
0:00
Step 5 of 6 — Breathe Through
Slow your exhale to lengthen the return. Breathe in for 4 counts. Hold for 2. Out for 6. Do this three times before moving on.
Step 6 of 6 — Reorient
Return to the present moment deliberately. Name three things you can see right now. Take one more slow breath. Notice whether the urge has changed in intensity since you started.

You surfed the urge.

You observed it, stayed with it, and let it pass without acting on it. That is the practice — and it gets easier every time you do it.

Cost-Benefit Analysis (CBA)
Your CBA
How it works
You get something from every behavior you engage in — that's why you did it. The CBA makes you examine both sides honestly: what you gain from the behavior and what you lose, both by continuing and by stopping.
Reflection Prompts
  • What benefits of the behavior surprised you?
  • What costs have you been avoiding looking at?
  • What would your life look like in one year if nothing changes?
Read the guide
You get something from every behavior you engage in — that's why you did it. The CBA makes you examine both sides honestly: what you gain from the behavior and what you lose, both by continuing and by stopping.
Why It Works
  • Compares short-term and long-term effects of behavior without pressure or shame
  • Surfaces the pattern that rewards tend to be short-lived while costs accumulate
  • Makes motivation visible — you can see what actually matters to you
  • Revisit it any time your resolve wobbles
4-Quadrant Framework
1
When I DO the behavior — Benefits
  • It can be fun
  • I connect with people
  • I avoid uncomfortable emotions
2
When I DO the behavior — Costs
  • I lose money
  • I break trust with others
  • I feel worse afterward
3
When I DON'T do the behavior — Benefits
  • I sleep better
  • I feel proud of myself
  • I move toward my goals
4
When I DON'T do the behavior — Costs
  • I might miss the social aspect
  • I'll have to deal with hard emotions
Label Each Item: Short-Term (ST) or Long-Term (LT)

Go back through all four quadrants. Mark each item ST or LT. Key insight: the benefits of using tend to be ST; the costs tend to be LT. The benefits of stopping tend to be LT — which is why they're easy to discount in the moment.

Reflection Prompts
  • What surprised you about your answers?
  • Which long-term benefits of stopping feel most important to you?
  • Are you trading long-term values for short-term comfort?
  • What's one small action this reflection points to?

The ABCs + D and E
Your ABC Log
How it works
The ABCDE model maps how external events trigger internal beliefs that produce emotional consequences — and gives you a process to dispute those beliefs and replace them with more effective ones. Recurring patterns across entries point to your specific cognitive tendencies — the ones worth focusing on.
Read the guide
Rooted in Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy (REBT). Urges rarely come from nowhere — they're connected to how you interpret events. The ABC tool breaks down the chain from situation to reaction so you can intervene at the belief level.
The Full Framework
A
Activating Event

What happened just before the urge or reaction? What was the situation? Was anything stressful, upsetting, or triggering?

B
Beliefs

What were you telling yourself? What thoughts came up automatically? Did you have any "musts" or "shoulds"? What did you believe about yourself, the situation, or others?

C
Consequences

What happened emotionally and physically? How did you feel? What was the urge? Did you act on it or resist it?

D
Dispute the Belief

Was that belief helpful or even true? Is this thought always accurate? What's another way to see the situation? What might a friend say to you?

E
Effective New Belief

Write a new belief that's more balanced and useful — honest, not toxic positivity. How do you feel thinking this new thought? What would you do differently with it?

Full Worked Example
A: My boss yelled at me in front of coworkers.
B: He has no right to embarrass me. It's not fair. I can't stand this.
C: I'm furious and want a drink.
D: Does my boss only yell at me? Is life always fair? Can I actually stand this without drinking?
E: My boss yells at everyone eventually. That felt bad, but it's over. He's not worth giving up my goals for.
Reflection Prompts
  • What part of this process felt most helpful?
  • How do urges feel when you stop to examine the underlying belief?
  • What's one situation this week where you could try the ABC tool?

Five Questions
Your Check-In
How it works
Five pointed questions that help you evaluate whether what you're currently doing actually serves what you actually want. Use it as a regular check-in — weekly works well — to stay connected to your intentions.
Read the guide
A structured check-in for when you're stuck, just had a setback, or need to realign. Five direct questions surface the gap between what you're doing and what you want — and what you can do about it.
The Five Questions
1
What do I want for my future?

What kind of life do you want? What goals or values matter most right now? Examples: be a present parent, feel healthy, live with more freedom, finish school.

2
What am I doing to get what I want?

List current actions. Are they helping or hurting? Examples: attending meetings, avoiding triggers — vs. skipping sleep, isolating, using when stressed.

3
How do I feel about what I'm doing now?

Be honest. Do your current actions feel aligned with what you want? Examples: frustrated, hopeful, discouraged, guilty, neutral, proud.

4
What could I do differently?

One or two small things to try. Examples: reach out to someone, ask for help, go for a walk instead of using, write out a plan.

5
How would changing what I do make me feel?

Imagine taking even a small step forward. Examples: lighter, more in control, relieved, motivated, proud.

The Key Comparison

Compare Q3 (how you feel now) with Q5 (how you'd feel with a change). The gap between those two answers is your motivation. Can that difference move you to act? Can the ideas from Q4 replace what you're trying to change?

Reflection Prompts
  • Which of the five questions stood out the most?
  • Did anything surprise you about your answers?
  • What's one small change you're willing to try this week?
  • How might you use these questions in future moments of stress or uncertainty?

Practice
Practice Self-Compassion
Shame and self-judgment don't drive lasting change — they drive relapse. This tool, based on Dr. Kristin Neff's research, gives you a concrete practice for meeting hard moments with care instead of attack.
3 Pillars (Neff Model)
1
Self-Kindness vs. Self-Judgment

Talk to yourself the way you'd talk to a friend. Reduce name-calling and blame. Try: "I'm doing the best I can" or "This is hard, and I still deserve support." Ask: would I say this to someone I care about?

2
Common Humanity vs. Isolation

You are not uniquely broken. Struggle is universal. Attend a mutual support meeting, talk with someone who understands, reflect on how others have faced setbacks and kept going.

3
Mindfulness vs. Over-Identification

Notice emotions without being consumed by them. Take a few deep breaths and name what you're feeling. Go for a walk and listen to what's around you. Practice brief grounding or meditation moments.

Reflection Prompts
  • How do you usually talk to yourself when things go wrong? Would you speak that way to someone you care about?
  • Which pillar feels most natural? Which feels most difficult?
  • Can you name a recent moment where you could have given yourself more compassion?
  • What might change if you offered encouragement instead of criticism after setbacks?
  • What's one small self-compassion practice you can try this week?
Framework
Put DENTS in Your Urges
A structured acronym for in-the-moment urge management. Use it when an urge is active and you need an immediate intervention framework.
The DENTS Framework
D
Deny or Delay

Refuse to give in, no matter what. If full refusal feels impossible, delay by just one moment at a time. Remind yourself as many times as needed that the urge will pass.

E
Escape

If you know what's triggering the urge, leave immediately or end the trigger. Leave the place, conversation, or social setting that's intensifying it.

N
Neutralize

Attack it and prove it wrong, or acknowledge it and let it pass. Create emotional distance: watch the urge build, crest, and fade. It doesn't define you — it's separate from you.

T
Tasks

Give yourself something to do. Urges need oxygen — deprive them by engaging your mind. Call a friend, count, recite something, start a task. Urges can't easily compete with active attention.

S
Swap

Change your internal monologue. Replace "This urge will kill me" with "This urge will pass." If you feel sad or lonely, deliberately change it: go to a coffee shop, take a walk, attend a SMART meeting.

Core Principle

DENTS isn't about eliminating urges — it's about developing a structured, sustainable way to manage them. The goal is not to get rid of urges completely but to stop being automatically controlled by them.

Practice
Personify and Disarm
The urge is not you. It's a temporary impulse shaped by past habits and stress. Giving it a name and treating it as an external manipulator creates enough mental distance to pause and choose differently.
3-Step Process
1
Name the Urge

If this urge had a personality, what would it be? Give it a name without judgment. Examples: The Smooth Talker, The Whiner, The Hurt Child, The Negotiator, The Saboteur.

2
Identify Its Scripts

What does this urge try to convince you of? Write down its lines. Examples: "You've earned this." / "This is too hard — you can't do it." / "Everyone else does it." / "You're already messed up, so why stop now?"

3
Talk Back

Write what you want to say back to reclaim control. Say it aloud, write it, or repeat it internally when the urge arrives. Examples: "You're lying to me again — I'm not listening." / "You're not in charge — I am." / "You're not worth trading my goals for."

When External Pressure Is Involved
  • Make eye contact — show you're serious
  • Speak in a firm, calm, confident voice
  • Don't feel guilty — you have the right to choose
  • After saying no, change the subject. Don't keep defending your choice.
Reflection Prompts
  • What name fits your most common urge?
  • What does that urge try to convince you of — and how can you respond?
  • How does it feel to treat the urge as something separate from yourself?
  • When might this strategy help you stay on track this week?

Setting Healthy Boundaries
Boundaries protect your time, energy, and recovery. They're not walls — they're fences that make relationships more workable and reduce the stress and resentment that fuel urges.
Key Concepts
  • Boundaries are about your needs, not controlling others
  • They can be physical, emotional, or behavioral
  • You have the right to protect your peace and stay connected on your terms
  • If someone pushes back, it doesn't mean your boundary is wrong — it means it's working
Common Challenges (Identify Yours)
  • Fear of conflict or rejection
  • Feeling selfish for saying no
  • Not knowing how to phrase it clearly
The Boundary Formula
"I feel" (name the emotion)
"When" (describe the specific behavior)
"Can I ask you to" (make the reasonable request)?
Example
"I feel frustrated when plans change last minute. Can I ask you to let me know earlier next time?"
Practice Step

Think of a real situation where you need a boundary. Fill in: What's happening that doesn't feel okay? How are you feeling? What would make you feel more respected or supported? Then use the formula above.

Reflection Prompts
  • How did it feel to write or say your boundary out loud?
  • What do you think might happen if you try this in real life?
  • What's one small boundary you can practice setting this week?

Unhelpful Thinking Styles — Quick Reference
The ABC and Dispute Unhelpful Beliefs tools both require you to identify the type of unhelpful thought you're having. This reference card maps the most common patterns, their signal words, and what they look like in practice — use it alongside those tools.
All-or-Nothing Thinking
always · never · completely · total failure
Seeing things in black and white with no middle ground. One mistake means total failure; one slip means recovery is over.
"I missed one meeting — I've completely fallen off the wagon."
Catastrophizing
terrible · worst · can't handle · disaster
Assuming the worst possible outcome is certain. Magnifies difficulties until they feel unsurvivable.
"If I feel this urge, I'm definitely going to give in and ruin everything."
Overgeneralization
always · everyone · nothing ever · every time
Drawing sweeping conclusions from a single event. One negative experience becomes an endless pattern.
"This always happens to me. I never get a break."
Mind Reading
they think · I know she · he must · they probably
Assuming you know what others are thinking — usually something negative — without actual evidence.
"Everyone at the meeting could tell I was struggling. They're judging me."
Emotional Reasoning
I feel it · it must be true · I feel like · therefore it is
Treating feelings as facts. Because you feel hopeless, you conclude the situation is hopeless.
"I feel like a failure, so I must be one."
Should Statements
I should · I must · I ought to · I have to
Rigid rules about how you or others must behave. Violations generate guilt, shame, or anger rather than motivation.
"I should be stronger than this. I shouldn't even be having urges."
Labeling
I'm a · I am such a · I'm just a
Attaching a global negative label to yourself or others based on a specific behavior. More extreme than overgeneralization.
"I'm an addict. That's just who I am. I can't change it."
Frustration Intolerance
can't stand · unbearable · I can't take · too much
Believing you cannot tolerate discomfort, even temporarily. Underestimates your actual capacity to endure difficulty.
"I can't stand feeling this way. I need relief right now."
How to use this
  • When an urge or difficult emotion hits, write the thought down first (the B in ABC).
  • Scan this list for the pattern that matches — naming it creates distance from it.
  • Then move to Dispute Unhelpful Beliefs: write the challenge question, then the more helpful belief.
  • Recurring patterns across entries point to your specific cognitive tendencies — the ones worth focusing on.

Dispute Unhelpful Beliefs
Beliefs can be changed. You don't have to believe everything you think. This tool identifies the specific patterns of unhelpful thinking that fuel addictive behavior, then gives you a systematic practice for replacing them.
Common Unhelpful Belief Patterns
TypeSignal WordsThe Problem
Demands"I must," "I have to"Creates pressure and emotional distress
Overgeneralizations"Always," "never," "only"Misses the nuance of real life
Frustration intolerance"I can't handle this"Underestimates your resilience
Awfulizations"Worst," "most unfair," "stupidest"Exaggerates pain and difficulty

3-Step Disputing Process (Use the Worksheet)
1
Write the Unhelpful Belief

What thought is getting in your way right now?

2
Ask a Challenge Question

Is this always true? Would I say this to a friend? What's another way of seeing this?

3

Replace with something honest, balanced, and supportive of your long-term goals — not toxic positivity, not denial, just a more accurate view.

When Things Get Hard — Lapse, Relapse, and Starting Again
This section is for when you've slipped, or feel like you're about to. It does not replace the other tools — it uses them. Read this before concluding anything about what the slip means.
The Difference Between a Lapse and a Relapse

These are not the same thing. The distinction matters because treating a lapse as a relapse is itself a mechanism that turns it into one.

Lapse
A single episode or brief return to a behavior. It has a beginning and an end. It is an event — not a verdict. Most people in recovery experience at least one. It is information about what needs more work, not evidence that recovery is impossible.
Relapse
A return to the prior pattern over time — a sustained reversion rather than an isolated incident. Relapses typically develop from lapses that were responded to with shame, secrecy, and abandonment of recovery practices — not from the lapse itself.

"A lapse is a detour, not a destination. You don't have to go back to the beginning. You pick up exactly where you left off."

The slip does not erase the work. The tools, the insights, the days of practice — all of that is still yours.
The Shame Spiral — How a Lapse Becomes a Relapse

Understanding this mechanism is one of the most protective things you can do. The slip itself is rarely the problem. What happens next is.

1
The lapse occursA single use, a single episode. In isolation, recoverable.
↓
Shame activates"I'm weak. I've failed. All that work was pointless. I'm hopeless."
← Interrupt here: this is emotional reasoning. The thought is not a fact. Use Dispute Unhelpful Beliefs.
↓
Withdrawal from supportToo ashamed to attend meetings, call support people, or use tools. The isolation removes the only things that help.
← Interrupt here: contact one person. Shame cannot survive disclosure.
↓
The abstinence violation effect"I've already broken it — might as well continue." All-or-nothing thinking turns a small slip into full permission.
← Interrupt here: this is the lapse→relapse mechanism. Recognize it by name.
↓
Pattern re-establishesRelapse — not because the first slip was inevitable, but because no intervention interrupted the spiral.
What to Do in the Next 10 Minutes — STOP
S
Stop
Don't act further. Don't make any decisions right now. Physical stillness first.
T
Take a breath
Five slow exhales. Physiologically shifts from threat response to thinking mode.
O
Observe
Name what's happening without judgment. "I just slipped. I'm feeling shame. I'm not in danger."
P
Proceed
Choose one action from the sequence below. One deliberate step forward.
Which Tools to Use After a Slip — In Order
1
Self-Compassion (Point 1)

Before analysis, before planning, before anything — meet the moment with self-kindness. The shame response impairs thinking. You cannot reason your way out of a shame spiral. Self-compassion creates the psychological safety to actually look at what happened.

2
ABC Exercise (Point 3)

Map what actually happened. A = the situation that preceded the slip. B = what you were telling yourself. C = what you felt and did. This is information, not a confession. You're building a clearer picture of your triggers and beliefs so they have less power next time.

3
Dispute Unhelpful Beliefs (Point 3)

Write down the shame thoughts that are arising and challenge each one. "I'm hopeless" → Is that always true? Have I made progress? What would I say to a friend? Replace each with a more accurate belief.

4
Cost-Benefit Analysis (Point 1)

Return to your CBA. Remind yourself of the long-term costs of the behavior and the long-term benefits of stopping. This is not about guilt — it's about reconnecting with why you started. The reasons haven't changed.

5
Update Your Change Plan (Point 1)

The slip revealed something about your plan — a gap in support, an unmanaged trigger, a high-risk situation that needs a specific strategy. Update the plan. This is what the plan is for.

6
Reach out — to a person, not a tool

Tell one person what happened. A SMART meeting, a trusted friend, a counselor. Shame is dismantled by disclosure. If you find yourself resisting this step above all others, that resistance is the thing most worth examining.

Recovery is not a straight line. The people who build lasting change are not the ones who never slip — they're the ones who respond to slips without abandoning the work.
